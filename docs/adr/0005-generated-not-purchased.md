# ADR-0005 — Every asset is generated or free; nothing is bought

**Status:** Accepted
**Date:** 2026-09-08
**Related:** ADR-0004, `docs/asset-licensing.md`, `docs/quality-bar.md`

## Context

The plan's stated target is a technical model that holds up next to Star
Citizen. Star Citizen's art is roughly a thousand people for twelve years, and
its ships and interiors are hand-modelled. That is the part of the comparison
that cannot be reached by being clever, because it is not a hard problem — it
is a large one.

The owner has ruled out buying assets. Stated 2026-09-08, no budget, not a
negotiating position.

So the art direction now rests entirely on two things: what code can generate,
and what is available under a licence that costs nothing. That is a constraint
on the *architecture*, not a preference about sourcing, because it decides what
the asset tasks in six milestones actually mean. "Ship art pipeline" means
something different when there is no artist at the other end of it.

## Decision

**Content is generated by rule, or it comes from a source that is free and
whose licence has been read. There is no third option, and no task may assume
an artist.**

Three categories, and they are not the same problem:

**1. Generated.** Terrain, surfaces, rocks, vegetation, buildings, ship
exteriors, ship and station interiors, cockpits, props, decals, wear. All of it
by rule. This is most of what fills a frame, and it is the category where code
is genuinely competitive — a panel-line decomposition or a curvature-driven
wear mask is a better fit for a program than for a person.

**2. Free and licensed.** Rigged characters and animation data. This is where
generation fails: a convincing walk cycle is captured or authored, and neither
is something a rule produces. Sourced the way surfaces were — read the licence,
record it in a manifest, fail the build on anything unaccounted for.

**3. Not attempted.** Hero props that need someone's taste, and soft goods.
Named here so their absence is a decision rather than a discovery.

## Why

**Because the alternative is a permanent placeholder.** The settlement in every
capture is thirty-two white boxes and three hundred cones. That is not a
stylistic choice, it is the absence of a generator, and it will stay until one
exists. Waiting for a budget that is not coming is how a project spends four
years with placeholder content.

**Because generated content compounds and bought content does not.** A building
generator that improves makes every building in the game better, forever. A
purchased building is one building. The same generator improves the settlements
in M12, the cities in M19 and the stations in M10, which is three milestones
paid for once.

**Because most of what reads as expensive is procedural anyway.** Panel breaks,
edge wear from curvature, grime accumulating by ambient occlusion, streaking
below seams, heat discolouration near thrusters, registration markings. Studios
generate these. The gap between a student project and a shipped one is mostly
this layer, and it is mask arithmetic.

**Because the cockpit is better generated than authored.** `docs/quality-bar.md`
promises that every gauge traces to a real component. A hand-authored cockpit
can lie about that; one laid out from the ship's component graph cannot. The
constraint turns out to buy something.

## What this costs

**It is slower.** A generator that produces good buildings is a great deal more
work than importing good buildings, and the first several versions will not be
good. That cost is accepted.

**Characters will be visibly weaker than everything else.** They come from a
free library, so they will not match a bespoke look, and every scene with a
person in it is judged on the person. Known, accepted, not solved.

**Repetition is the failure mode to design against, not patch later.** The
characteristic tell of generated content is the same corridor five times and
the same rock nineteen times. Variation is a framework task, scheduled as one.

**The M11 gate becomes a much bigger bet.** "A stranger cannot immediately sort
ours from theirs on material and lighting alone" was always ambitious; against
generated geometry it is the single hardest promise in the plan. It stays, and
if it is going to fail it should fail loudly there.

## The judgement problem, stated plainly

Generation is iteration, and iteration needs someone who can tell whether the
result is good. Three times in one session the assistant misread its own
captures — a camera underground, an exposure in the wrong units, a camera
standing inside a building — and each time believed the render before believing
the arithmetic.

So the human is the art director and the machine is the modeller, and the
review loop is infrastructure rather than a nicety. The turntable harness is
scheduled **before** the first generator that needs it, for the same reason the
capture comparison was built before the material work: a fast, honest way to
look at the result is the thing that makes a hundred bad iterations affordable.

## Consequences

- `docs/asset-licensing.md` covers all three categories, not only surfaces.
- Every asset task in M02, M05, M09, M10, M11 and M12 is a generator task or a
  sourcing task. No task imports authored art.
- The manifest-and-validator discipline built for surfaces extends to
  characters and animation.
- If the position on buying ever changes, this ADR is superseded rather than
  quietly ignored, because a bought asset with no manifest entry fails the
  build.
